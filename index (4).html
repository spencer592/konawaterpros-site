Why filtered water, and why not just a pitcher

Pitchers and refrigerator filters are mostly designed for chlorine taste and odor, plus whichever contaminants are listed for that specific cartridge. They are usually not reverse osmosis, so do not assume they reduce fluoride, nitrate, sodium or dissolved solids.

Reverse osmosis membranes are extremely fine, with openings far smaller than most microplastic particles. That is why RO is often discussed as a way to reduce them, though results for any specific system depend on its own testing.

Whatever you choose, look for certified contaminant claims, and replace filters on schedule.

How much water do you need?

The National Academies of Sciences, Engineering, and Medicine puts adequate total water intake at about 3.7 liters (15.5 cups) a day for men and 2.7 liters (11.5 cups) for women. That counts water from food and all beverages, and roughly 20% usually comes from food.

Needs rise with body size, activity and heat, so active people in Hawaii’s climate often need more. Since you drink this much water every day, where it comes from matters.

Do the math on a 5-gallon jug

A 2024 study in PNAS found about 240,000 plastic particles per liter of bottled water on average, mostly nanoplastics, across three popular brands (110,000 to 370,000 depending on the brand).

A 5-gallon jug holds about 19 liters. At that average, the math works out to roughly 4.5 million micro- and nanoplastic particles per jug, if jugs behave like the bottles tested. Delivered jugs were not part of that study, so this is an estimate, not a measurement.
